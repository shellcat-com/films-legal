Free Wedding Photo Sharing: 7 Options and Their Catches

Seven ways to share wedding photos for $0, and what each one quietly costs you.

Filmsera· November 16, 2026 ·9 min read

Short answer: The best free options for wedding photo sharing are a Google Photos shared album (if guests use Google), an iCloud Shared Album (a temporary one costs no storage), a Dropbox file request for big files, and a guest-photo app’s free tier for small weddings. Each is free only up to a point: storage caps, sign-ins, compression and privacy are the catches.

Every free option charges you in something other than money: your storage, your guests’ patience or your photo quality. Free wedding photo sharing websites and apps run on the same trade-off. If you aren’t paying, you are usually spending storage, accepting a guest cap or asking guests to have the right account.

The 7 free options and their catches

1. A Google Photos shared album

The limit: each Google Account includes up to 15 GB of storage, shared across Gmail, Google Drive and Google Photos. Guests need a Google Account to add photos, and their uploads go into their own accounts. When you save their photos into your own library, those copies count against your 15 GB.

Who it suits: guest lists where nearly everyone already uses Gmail or an Android phone.

The hidden cost: sign-in friction at the table, and an album anyone with the link can open. If you stop sharing it later, Google removes the photos guests added. Our Google Photos shared album guide covers the setup and the fixes.

2. An iCloud Shared Album

The limit: iCloud comes with 5 GB of free storage. Since iOS 27, new shared albums keep full resolution and count toward the owner’s iCloud storage, including every photo participants add, with a cap of 100 participants, owner included. Albums made on older software don’t count toward storage but shrink photos to 2,048 pixels on the long edge.

The free workaround: a temporary shared album doesn’t count toward your storage and holds up to 5,000 photos and videos from all contributors. Apple deletes a temporary album after 30 days, so save everything you want before then, and temporary albums aren’t offered in every country or region.

Who it suits: iPhone hosts. Android guests are no longer left out, because new albums let people join and contribute on the web, even without an Apple Account.

The hidden cost: a regular album can quietly fill a free iCloud plan and stop your phone backing up. Our iCloud shared album guide walks through the settings.

3. A Google Drive folder

The limit: the same 15 GB per Google Account. Files in a shared folder count against the storage of whoever uploaded them, not yours, so guests spend their own space. People without a Google Account can’t upload into a regular Drive folder, and a Google Form with a file-upload question also makes them sign in.

Who it suits: the friend with a real camera, or a family that already lives in Google Drive. It keeps original files, video included.

The hidden cost: nobody uploads to Drive from a dance floor, so you are back to chasing people afterward, and the folder needs tidying once 40 people have dumped files into it.

4. A Dropbox file request

The limit: anyone can upload to a file request without a Dropbox account, but every file lands in your Dropbox and uses your space, and Dropbox Basic, the free plan, offers 2 GB. When you run out, guests see an error instead of an upload.

Who it suits: collecting a handful of big files after the wedding: the videographer cousin, the drone footage, the friend who shot film and had it scanned.

The hidden cost: 2 GB goes quickly once full-size photos and videos arrive. Treat it as a backstop for a few people, not the plan for 120 guests.

5. A WhatsApp group (or any group chat)

The limit: it’s free, but chat apps compress photos by default. WhatsApp offers an HD option, and each sender has to pick it, or set it as their default, for it to apply.

Who it suits: families spread across countries who already share everything in one group, and only for a few favorites each.

The hidden cost: photos get buried between messages, everyone in the group sees everything, and saving hundreds of photos from a chat is slow, manual work.

6. The free tier of a guest-photo app

Guest-photo apps are the only option on this list built for weddings: one QR code, one shared roll, and no chasing afterward. 7. Asking guests to send photos after the wedding

The limit: none, except attention. Depending on the app, photos sent by text or email can arrive downsized, and you’ll be saving them one message at a time.

Who it suits: everyone, as a backstop. Send one message with one link within 48 hours, and follow up once.

The hidden cost: it usually collects the fewest photos, because people mean to send them and then don’t.

How to share wedding photos with guests for free

Collecting is half the job. Sharing the finished set back is the other half, and it costs nothing:

  • A view-only album. In Google Photos, turn Collaborate off when uploads are done and send the same link. Guests can browse it without adding anything.
  • Your iCloud album link. Everyone who joined keeps access, so a short text saying “it’s all in there” does the job.
  • Your photographer’s gallery. If your photographer delivered an online gallery, add that link to the same message so guests get both sets at once.
  • A recap. A short video of the night, shared in the group chat, is easy to watch for guests who won’t open an album.

How to keep it free after the wedding

  1. Download everything within a week into one folder on your computer, then back it up to an external drive.
  2. Don’t save a second copy into the same cloud. Saving guests’ photos into your own library uses your storage and can push you into a paid plan.
  3. Watch the deadlines: temporary iCloud albums, free trials and free storage periods all end. Put the date in your calendar.
  4. Delete the duplicates before you pay for space you don’t need.
  5. Print the twenty you love most. It is the cheapest backup there is.

FAQ

What is the best free wedding photo sharing app?

For most couples it is the one their guests can use without signing up for anything. Google Photos and iCloud are free and familiar but weren’t built for weddings, and each needs either a Google Account to upload or the host’s iCloud storage. Guest-photo apps are built for the job, and their free tiers cover small weddings: Filmsera hosts up to 10 guests for free.

How can I share wedding photos for free without guests installing an app?

Use something that works in a phone’s browser. A Dropbox file request takes uploads without an account, new iCloud shared albums let anyone contribute on the web, and some guest-photo apps, Filmsera included, let guests join and shoot from their phone’s browser with no app or account. Google Photos needs a Google Account to add photos.

Is Google Photos free for wedding photos?

Yes, within storage limits. Each Google Account includes up to 15 GB, shared with Gmail and Drive. Guests’ uploads use their own storage, so a guest whose Google storage is full may not be able to add photos. Copies you save into your own library use yours. If you plan to keep everything in Google Photos long term, check how much free space you have first.

Do iCloud shared albums count against my storage?

Albums created on iOS 27 or later do: they keep full resolution and count every photo, including guests’ uploads, toward the owner’s iCloud storage. Albums made on older software don’t count but shrink photos to 2,048 pixels on the long edge; upgrade one and only photos added afterward count. Temporary shared albums don’t count toward storage and are deleted after 30 days, so save what you want first.

Can I make a free QR code for wedding photo sharing?

Yes. A Google Photos shared album can show its own QR code, QR generators can make static codes for free, and Filmsera’s invite card includes one. Avoid free trials of dynamic codes, which some providers switch off when the trial ends. Print the code at least 2 inches wide on table cards and test it on an iPhone and an Android phone.
